# WEAVE Lab presentation rules

Source: https://youtongzheng.github.io/handbook/#presentations (Youtong Zheng's group, University of Houston).

## Why they matter

Every rule below comes from two ideas.

**A talk is communication, not a judgment.** Many students treat a talk as being judged, so they try to prove themselves by showing everything they did: many slides, many figures, every detail. That confuses the audience. A talk is you and your audience working together on a bigger question. Ask "What do they need to understand?", not "How do I look?" Put the effort into your everyday work; in the talk, keep only what matters most.

**Your audience owes you nothing.** Most of the time, they don't want to listen to you. Unless they are paid to be there, as a committee is, the only reason they stay is that your talk is worth their time. Every slide has to earn its place.

## The rules, in order of importance

### 1. One slide, one message

Why: show everything, and you show nothing. A crowded slide means you don't know your point yet.

- Each slide makes one point.
- The slide title states that point as a full sentence (e.g., "Large storms are too dark in the model"), not a topic word ("Results").
- The body is the evidence, usually one figure. Use as little text as possible; no paragraphs and no long bullet lists.
- If a slide carries two messages, split it into two slides.

### 2. Big figures, big fonts

Why: stand in the audience's seat. Design for the back row, not for your laptop.

- Figures fill the slide. Do not paste multi-panel paper figures with tiny labels.
- Axis labels, tick labels, and legends are large; lines are thick.
- Remove clutter: unneeded gridlines, legends, and decimals.
- When panels are compared, use the same color scale and map size.

### 3. Build it up, tell a story

Why: storytelling is the core of a good talk. Reveal one piece at a time.

- Show only what the speaker is talking about now; hide the rest.
- Add one element at a time (e.g., first the axes and observations, then the model, then an arrow on the difference).
- The talk follows a story: question, what we did, what we found, why it matters. Each slide sets up the next.

### 4. Bonus: hook first, end on the takeaway

Not required, but it makes a good talk better. Why: if you can say it in one sentence, you know what you want to convey.

- If possible, show the key takeaway near the start to catch attention.
- End with a takeaway slide that repeats the 1–3 key messages, not "Thank you" or "Questions?"
- The hook and the takeaway carry the same message.

## Instructions for an AI agent reviewing slides

When the user asks you to review a slide deck against these rules:

1. Read every slide (titles, text, figures, and speaker notes if available).
2. For each slide, check rules 1 and 2. Report the slide number, the rule broken, and a concrete fix (e.g., a rewritten sentence title, which panels to drop, which labels to enlarge).
3. For the whole deck, check rules 3 and 4: Is there a clear story? Which slides should be built up step by step? Rule 4 is a bonus, not a requirement: suggest a hook or a takeaway slide, but do not count their absence as a failure.
4. Point out slides that seem to be there only to show effort and do not support the main message, and say so gently if the deck looks too long for its time slot.
5. State the talk's main message in one sentence. If you cannot, say so: the deck may not have one yet.
6. Rank your suggestions by the order of the rules above. Be brief and specific.
